Concepts

Six ideas hold the whole integration together. Read this page once and the rest of the documentation stops being a list of settings.

The chain

Every energy device in the house is a cell. Cells are lined up in a chain, and the chain always ends at the grid.

solar→ house→ car charger→ battery→ battery→ grid

All cells share one interface: same inputs, same outputs. Solar is not a special case in the code, and neither is the grid — they are simply cells whose behaviour is fixed rather than configurable. That uniformity is why adding a heat pump or a second charger later requires no changes to anything that already exists.

Each tick, the chain is walked twice.

PassDirectionCarriesMeaning
Downstream solar → grid rest (W), forecast (Wh) What is left over after everything before me has taken its share.
Upstream grid → solar headroom (W), current_power (W) How much room is left before the grid limit is hit.

A cell sees only its two direct neighbours. There is no global state and no central optimiser deciding everything at once.

If your grid sensor reports import as negative, do not invert it in the integration — invert it in a template sensor and point Energy Balancer at that. Half the confusing behaviour reported against tools like this one comes from a sign that is wrong in one place and right in another.

Position is priority

Every cell has a position: a small integer that decides where it sits in the chain. Lower numbers are served first. Solar, house and grid occupy fixed positions; your own devices go in between, and the order you choose is the policy.

Put the car charger before the home battery and surplus fills the car first. Swap them and the battery fills first, and the car only gets what is left over. There is no separate priority setting — position is it.

Equal position means a pool

Give two cells the same position and they stop being competitors. They become one pool (also called a layer or tier): a single virtual battery with one shared state of charge, one shared target, and one decision.

Whatever the pool is told to do is split between its members in proportion to how much room each one still has — remaining capacity, being the distance from the current state of charge to its maximum, multiplied by the cell's capacity in kWh. The battery that is emptiest, and biggest, takes the largest share.

Pooling only happens when every cell at that position is in surplus or balanced mode. Force one member to charge and the pool dissolves: the members fall back to being handled one after the other, in the plain chain order. A pool with a forced member is not a pool any more.

Mode and action are separate

This is the distinction that everything else depends on.

Every combination is valid and meaningful. A cell set to autonomous — meaning Energy Balancer keeps its hands off entirely — can still be observed to discharge 200 W. That is not a contradiction; there is no command to display, so the only honest thing to show is the measurement.

Structural modes

Set by the integration, not configurable:

ModeCellBehaviour
supplysolarAdds its measured power to rest.
demandhouseSubtracts its measured power from rest.
gridnetAbsorbs whatever remains, bounded by the import limit.

Configurable modes

Chosen per cell, in the setup wizard or the options flow:

ModeMeaning
surplus Charge only when there is a surplus; discharge when there is a deficit. The everyday self-consumption behaviour.
balanced Charge or discharge against a dynamic target state of charge, derived from the solar forecast and recent consumption, with hysteresis so it does not oscillate around the target.
charge Forced charging, from the grid if necessary.
discharge Forced discharging, even while there is a surplus.
autonomous The cell governs itself. Energy Balancer measures it and accounts for it in the chain, but sends it nothing.
off Not steered. Still measured, and its measured power still affects rest — an unsteered load is real.

What a forced mode may never override. charge and discharge are strong, not absolute. They remain bounded by:

  • Peak headroom. A forced charge is limited to rest + headroom, so it cannot quietly push your monthly peak up.
  • The state-of-charge limits and their tapers, and take_pct.
  • no_discharge_on_flag, which is a safety interlock — for instance "never empty the house battery into the car" — not a preference a mode gets to ignore.

Legacy spellings

Two modes were renamed. self_consumption became surplus because it was a vendor's word for a work mode, not a description of behaviour; smart became balanced because it said nothing at all. Stored configurations holding the old names still load — they are translated on the way in — so nothing breaks on upgrade.

What actually reaches the hardware

The chain produces a decision, and the decision is deliberately tiny. Modes never reach your devices; the actuator does not need to know whether you chose balanced or surplus.

DecisionMeans
steer, power > 0charge at this many watts
steer, power < 0discharge at this many watts
steer, power = 0hold — actively do nothing
releaselet go; the cell regulates itself
stoptake no part

Hold and release are not the same thing, and on real hardware they are physically different states. "Hold" means the battery is actively pinned at zero. "Release" means its own regulator has the wheel back. Collapsing the two is the single most expensive mistake you can make in an actuator script: a battery told to release when it should hold will happily start charging from the grid.

Observer mode

With observer mode on — and it is on by default — the integration does everything except act. It reads sensors, runs the chain, publishes every entity, and calls nothing.

Leave it on for the first day. Watch whether the numbers match what your inverter app says. Only then turn it off. There is a switch, switch.eb_observer_mode, so you can flip it back the moment something looks wrong.

Peak protection

Many grid tariffs charge on the highest quarter-hour import of the month, which means a single careless moment sets a price you pay for a long time.

Energy Balancer treats the import limit as the chain's upper bound. It is the headroom that travels back up the chain, and it caps every cell's charge decision — including the forced ones. Supply the limit as a fixed number (peak_limit_w), or point at one or more sensors and the highest value wins.

Pointing at your own running monthly-peak sensor is a common pattern: once the peak for the month is already set, there is no reason to stay below it, so the limit follows it upward. Be aware of the consequence — the ceiling rises with the peak, so anything that guards against exceeding it starts guarding a higher number.

An external charge policy

Some setups already have something deciding whether the batteries should be charging — a tariff optimiser, a scheduler, a script of your own. Energy Balancer can defer to it instead of duplicating it.

Point charge_pct_sensor at a sensor whose state is a percentage and whose mode attribute carries the intent. The percentage alone is ambiguous: zero can mean "no room in the peak window", "my inputs dropped out" or "let go", and those demand different responses. The mode attribute disambiguates it.

mode attributestateUnderstood as
eco_charge> 0Charge, at this percentage of the pool's charge capacity.
eco_charge0Do not pull from the grid — but keep covering the house.
generalanythingHands off; the cells regulate themselves.
anything elseanythingNo external policy; Energy Balancer uses its own logic.

"Do not charge" is not "do nothing". A zero from the external policy means stop importing, never park the battery. Covering the house from storage is a separate decision. An actuator script that reads zero as "stand still" will leave the house drawing from the grid with a full battery sitting next to it — which is exactly the situation peak protection exists to prevent.
